Most guides on hiring a web developer are written by people who have never shipped a site under a deadline. This one isn't. I build websites and developer tools for a living, and I've been on both sides of this conversation: the one being hired, and the one cleaning up after someone who shouldn't have been.

Here's what actually separates a freelancer who will deliver from one who will leave you with a half-finished dashboard and a login you can't reset.

## Start with the work, not the résumé

A list of frameworks tells you almost nothing. Anyone can write "React, Next.js, Tailwind" on a profile. What you want is proof that those words turned into something real that people use.

Ask for two or three live links. Not screenshots, not a Figma file, but URLs you can open on your phone. Then poke at them. Is it fast? Does it break when you rotate your phone? Does the contact form actually send? A developer's portfolio is the one place they had full control and no excuses, so it's the most honest thing they'll show you.

## Watch how they scope the project

The first real test happens before any code exists. Give a rough description of what you need and listen to how they respond.

A weak freelancer says yes to everything and quotes a number in the first message. A strong one asks questions that make you think. Who's this for? What happens after someone lands on the page? Do you already have the content? That instinct to narrow the problem before pricing it is the single best predictor I know of a project that finishes on time.

## Insist on a small first milestone

Never hand over a full project on trust. Break it into pieces and make the first one small: a single page, a working prototype, one flow that runs end to end.

This protects both of you. You get to see how they communicate, how they handle feedback, and whether the thing they ship matches the thing they described. They get a clear, bounded target instead of a vague "build me a website." If the first milestone goes sideways, you've lost a week, not a quarter.

## Ask who owns everything at the end

This is the question people forget, and it's the one that hurts most later. Before you pay a deposit, get clear answers:

- Who owns the code and the domain when it's done?
- Where does the site get deployed, and is that account in your name?
- Can you edit the content yourself, or do you have to call them for every typo?

A developer who builds for the long term hands you the keys without being asked. One who's building themselves a dependency will be vague here. Push until it's concrete.

## Judge communication over credentials

You are not hiring a diploma. You're hiring someone you'll trade messages with for weeks, often while something is broken and you're stressed about it.

The freelancer who replies clearly, tells you the honest timeline, and says "I don't know yet, let me check" is worth more than the one with the longer client list and the one-word answers. Speed of reply during the sales conversation is usually the best version of the communication you'll get. It rarely improves after the invoice is paid.

## The quiet signals that matter

A few small things predict a good build more reliably than anything on a proposal:

- They send you a short recap after a call, so nothing lives only in memory.
- They mention performance and mobile without you bringing it up.
- They push back on a bad idea instead of silently building it.
- Their own website loads fast and works. The plumber's pipes should be in order.

None of these show up on a skills list. All of them show up in the first week.

## Before you sign

Hiring well is mostly about slowing down for one conversation and paying attention to how it feels. Look at real work, start small, get ownership in writing, and treat clear communication as the skill it is. Do that and you'll rarely end up in the rescue-job situation I described at the top.
