Most bad hires aren't obvious until month two. The developer answered emails fast, the first call went well, and the price sounded fair. Then the timeline slips, "almost done" turns into "one more week" three times in a row, and you're stuck mid-project with a half-built site and no easy way out.

None of that is unpredictable. It shows up early if you know where to look. Here's what actually matters before you sign anything.

## Start with the portfolio, not the pitch

Anyone can talk about "clean code" and "modern design." A portfolio either backs that up or it doesn't.

Open three of their live projects and check:

- Does the site actually load fast, or does it stall on a spinner?
- Does it work on your phone, not just their demo screenshots?
- Is there real variety, or is every project the same template with a new logo dropped in?

If a developer can't point you to live, working sites, treat that as the whole answer. Screenshots and Dribbble mockups are not proof anything ships.

## Ask about their stack, not just their price

You don't need to understand the technical details, but you do need to know two things: what they're building with, and who owns it when the project ends.

A developer who builds on a proprietary drag-and-drop platform locks you into their tooling, and often their availability, forever. A developer working in standard frameworks (Next.js, WordPress, whatever fits your case) hands you something any other developer can pick up later. Always ask directly: "If I needed someone else to take this over in a year, could they?" A confident yes is a good sign. Hesitation is the answer too.

## Fixed price vs. hourly: what you're actually paying for

Both models work. They just carry the risk differently.

| | Fixed scope | Hourly |
|---|---|---|
| **Best for** | A defined project with clear pages/features | Ongoing work, unclear scope, ops-heavy fixes |
| **Who carries the risk** | The developer, since they estimated the total | You, since hours can run over |
| **Change requests** | Priced separately, in writing | Just more hours |
| **Budget predictability** | High | Low to medium |

If your project has a clear start and end (a landing page, a portfolio site, a defined feature set), push for a fixed-scope quote. It forces the developer to actually think through the work before starting, and it protects your budget from drifting. Save hourly arrangements for maintenance and open-ended work, where scope genuinely can't be pinned down in advance.

## Watch how they handle scope changes

This is the single best predictor of how the whole project will go. Bring up a plausible mid-project change, something like "what if I want to add a booking form after the homepage is done?", and pay attention to the answer.

> A developer who says "let's scope that separately once we get there" is protecting the project. One who says "sure, no problem" to everything, with no mention of time or cost, is setting up a conversation you'll have to have later. On their terms, not yours.

Vague generosity upfront almost always turns into resentment or corner-cutting later, because nobody priced the extra work in the first place.

## Red flags that predict a rough project

- There's no written proposal. If the scope, price, and timeline only exist in a chat thread, there's nothing to hold either side to.
- There are no milestones. A project with a single "done" date at the end gives you zero visibility until it's too late to course-correct.
- They want the full fee upfront. Reasonable developers ask for a deposit, not the whole payment before work starts.
- They can't explain a decision from a past project: why they picked a certain approach, or what tradeoff it solved. If they can't, they may not have made the decision themselves.
- They don't ask you anything back. A developer who quotes a price without asking about your business, your users, or your timeline is quoting a template, not your project.

## Questions to ask on the first call

1. Can you show me two or three live sites you built, not just designed?
2. What happens if I need changes after the "final" version ships?
3. Who owns the code, the domain, and the hosting once we're done?
4. What's your process if something breaks after launch?
5. Can you put the scope, price, and timeline in writing before I pay anything?

If the answers are specific and consistent, you've likely found someone worth hiring. If they're vague, that vagueness doesn't go away once you've signed. It just becomes your problem instead of theirs.
